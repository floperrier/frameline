# The order of the ways on is written, not drawn

The Cuts leaving a Scene are offered to the Reader in an order the Author sets by
moving them earlier or later in the Scene's node — a Place the Cut carries, the
same numbering a Shot has in its Scene's run. The obvious alternative is to take
the order from the graph itself, so that a Cut's rank is the angle at which it
leaves its Scene, and reordering the ways on is a matter of dragging a node.

## Considered Options

Reading the order off the graph is the more elegant idea and we rejected it. A
Scene's `x` and `y` say nothing about the Story — nothing stops two Scenes from
sitting on top of each other, and nothing should, because the graph is a drawing
the Author arranges for their own sake. An order derived from it would make
dramaturgy a consequence of layout: tidying the graph would silently rewrite
which way on the Reader reads first, and two Scenes at the same point would leave
two Cuts with the same angle and no order at all. The first way on offered is
also the one that takes focus when a Scene plays out, so this is a decision about
what the Reader meets, not about how the drawing looks.

## Consequences

An Author who has arranged the graph so that a Cut visibly leaves to the left
still has to say, separately, that it comes first. The two can disagree, and
nothing warns about it. In exchange, the drawing stays free — it can be laid out
for legibility, or not at all — and a Story read at its public link offers its
ways on in exactly the order they were written in, whatever has happened to the
graph since.
